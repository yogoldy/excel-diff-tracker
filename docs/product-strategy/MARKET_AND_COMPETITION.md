# Market and competition

## Market assessment

There is a credible market-shaped gap, but no standalone market has yet been demonstrated. Adjacent
products prove that organizations pay for Excel preservation, workbook audit, scenario planning,
version control, and financial communication. They do not prove that enough customers will purchase
this exact combination.

The likely early user is an analyst who maintains a consequential, long-lived workbook; repeatedly
answers management what-if questions; spends material time validating and presenting results; and
cannot or does not want to migrate the model to an enterprise planning platform.

## Competitive map

### Research closely

- **PerfectXL:** workbook comparison, structural change detection, formula risk, visualization,
  documentation, and VBA comparison. Study its comparison UX, handling of structural shifts, audit
  exports, and product packaging.
- **Operis Analysis Kit (OAK):** serious financial-model review, formula tracing, model maps,
  comparison, reconstruction, and audit workflow. Study where it ends between model review and
  reusable scenario management.

These are the closest competitors if the product is framed as an Excel diff or audit tool. The
intended distinction is that their central object appears to be a workbook or workbook version,
whereas this product's central object would be a decision executed against a certified baseline.

### Strategic concerns

- **Cube:** preserves spreadsheet models and formulas while adding governed data, collaboration,
  planning, AI, and audit trails.
- **Datarails:** preserves Excel workflows while adding centralized data, consolidation, version
  history, planning, and reporting.

Both could plausibly extend into semantic scenario comparison. The potential wedge is a lighter,
local-first product requiring no broad FP&A implementation: identify existing controls and outputs,
run the existing workbook, and produce governed decision evidence.

### Broader enterprise alternative

- **Vena:** strongly Excel-oriented but fundamentally a broader planning-platform implementation.
  It competes when an organization is considering FP&A transformation, less directly when one
  analyst needs to operationalize a specialized existing workbook.

### Supplementary or different categories

- **Microsoft Spreadsheet Compare:** useful structural comparison, not one-workbook scenario
  management or decision explanation.
- **Copilot and Excel agents:** valuable analyst-supervised development and investigation tools, but
  not the trusted unattended calculation path.
- **ClusterSeven:** spreadsheet-estate governance and compliance; potentially complementary.
- **Workday Adaptive Planning and Anaplan:** their planning engines become authoritative, making them
  platform migration or replacement choices rather than direct existing-workbook runners.

This map is provisional research, not a claim that competitors lack unreviewed features.

## Search-led utility hypothesis

A simple multi-sheet scenario manager may be an unusually clear entry product. Candidate user intent
includes searches such as “Excel Scenario Manager multiple sheets,” “Excel scenario manager
alternative,” “save what-if scenarios across worksheets,” and “compare Excel scenarios.” Search
volume, conversion, and willingness to pay remain untested.

The concise promise is:

> Select inputs and outputs anywhere in the workbook. Save scenarios, compare results, restore the
> baseline, and export.

A low one-time price could test demand, but durable support, code signing, Excel compatibility, and
edge-case handling may require a higher paid tier or continuing revenue. Pricing is an experiment,
not a settled decision.

## Market validation questions

Before treating this as a software market, validate:

- How often do analysts perform consequential manual what-if runs?
- How many workbooks already contain useful driver logic?
- How much time is spent recording, checking, explaining, and reproducing each run?
- How frequently do stale inputs, accidental changes, or workbook copies cause problems?
- Will users install and pay for a local multi-sheet scenario manager?
- Will an analyst configure a second scenario after experiencing the first?
- Does the same organization want shared scenarios, controls, and approvals?
- How much customer-specific setup remains after the analyst identifies levers and outputs?

The strongest early evidence would be repeated use, payment for a pilot or utility, configuration of
additional scenarios, and requests to deploy the workflow to other analysts.
